01

How these were classified

There was no rating study, no participant panel, and no anchoring to published norms. I read the list of 89 sound IDs and assigned each one a valence and an arousal value from my own priors, in a single pass, applying roughly these rules:

Water, weather, soft naturepleasant, low arousal → v +0.4 to +0.8, a −0.2 to −0.5
Sirens, construction, heavy trafficunpleasant, high arousal → v −0.5 to −0.9, a +0.5 to +0.9
Machine and appliance humaffectively inert → v and a both near 0
Binaural beatsarousal scaled by band: delta −0.8 → gamma +0.7
Social spacesarousal by crowd density; valence by whether it reads as welcoming
Animalsvalence by domesticity, arousal by vocal abruptness

The rules were not written down first and then applied — I reconstructed them above by looking at what I had already done. That distinction matters, because it means they were never applied consistently, and the diagnostics in section 3 show exactly where the inconsistency landed.

What this map is not

It is not measurement. Every coordinate is one person's guess, produced quickly to make the transition engine runnable. Nothing here should be cited as a property of the sounds, and no study should ship on it. Section 5 covers what would have to replace it.

It collapsed into one dimension

Valence and arousal correlate at r = −0.715, sharing 51% of their variance, and a principal-components decomposition puts 85.8% of all spread on a single axis running at −44° — almost exactly the diagonal. In other words I did not rate two things. I rated one latent quality, roughly "how nice is this sound", and then split it across two axes that were never independent.

This is the failure that matters most for the study. A trajectory that resolves arousal first and valence second — the whole point of the iso-principle condition — assumes you can move along one axis while holding the other. On this map you cannot: there are almost no sounds off the diagonal to move through.

One quadrant is empty

Only two of 89 sounds land in the low-arousal, negative-valence region. That region is flat, listless, dreary — and it is one of the most common states a person actually opens an ambient sound app in. If a participant reports feeling that way, the engine has nothing to meet them with, which breaks the iso-principle condition before it starts.

Some of that is honest: Moodist is a wellbeing app and does not stock many dreary sounds. But not all of it. Clock, Dryer, Slide Projector, Laundry Room and Submarine all have a decent claim on that quadrant, and I parked every one of them near the origin instead.

The resolution is too coarse

Only 67 distinct positions for 89 sounds, drawn from 17 valence values and 16 arousal values — everything snapped to a 0.1 grid. Seventeen groups sit at identical coordinates, including three-way ties like Dryer / Slide Projector / Washing Machine and Wolf / Tuning Radio / Highway. Sounds at identical coordinates are interchangeable to the engine, so which one you hear is decided by object key order rather than by anything meaningful.

What replaces it

Two routes, and they are not exclusive:

  • Anchor to published norms. Match each Moodist sound to its nearest equivalent in IADS-E or Emo-Soundscapes and inherit those coordinates. Fast, citable, and immediately fixes the dimensional collapse, because those norms were derived from many raters rather than one. The weakness is match quality — Rain on Tent has no clean equivalent.
  • Run our own rating pass. Pairwise comparison rather than absolute rating: people are unreliable at "rate this 1–9 on pleasantness" but consistent at "which of these two is more pleasant". Fit a Bradley–Terry model to recover a scale. More defensible, and it becomes a contribution in its own right rather than a dependency.

Either way, three checks should gate the result before any participant sees it: valence and arousal correlating below roughly |0.3|, all four quadrants populated enough to render a mix, and inter-rater agreement reported. The current map fails the first two and cannot report the third.

Worth checking either way

Real affective-norm datasets do show some valence–arousal correlation, so the target is not zero. But −0.72 with 86% of variance on one axis is far past that, and the shape of the correlation in published norms should be compared against ours directly rather than assumed.
